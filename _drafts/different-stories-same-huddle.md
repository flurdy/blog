We were all remote. In my previous engagement, four or five of us would often work alongside each other in a Slack huddle, breaking for lunch.

We weren’t necessarily working on the same story. Sometimes we were. Often we each had something different underway. But we could hear each other, share screens, ask questions and chat while we worked.

People joined and left as it suited them.

I really liked it.

Recently, a longer, chattier standup in my new engagement reminded me of those huddles. Not because they were the same arrangement, but because there was room for conversation beyond a quick round of updates.

It got me thinking about what I liked about working that way.

## Coming together, spreading out

When something important needed attention, we swarmed on it. Everyone could get involved in understanding the problem, discussing an approach and looking at what came back.

For more routine work, we spread out again. People worked on their own stories, but stayed in the huddle. Sometimes sharing a screen, sometimes not.

Slack huddles helped because several people could share their screens at once. We didn’t have to organise everything around one person presenting while everyone else watched.

The amount of collaboration changed with the work. We didn’t need to choose between mobbing all day and working alone.

## Agents working, people learning

We were also setting AI agents to work.

That gave us plenty to discuss: how someone had framed a problem, what they had asked an agent to do, what it returned, and whether that was actually a useful answer.

Several agents could be getting on with the grunt work while we compared approaches and discussed results. Sometimes that pulled us onto the same problem. Sometimes it was a brief exchange before everyone returned to their own story.

I liked hearing how other people approached things. Otherwise, much of that thinking stays inside somebody else’s private conversation with an AI. You might eventually review the resulting code without ever hearing the discussion that led to it.

In the huddle, we could share that thinking as we went.

And not every conversation needed to improve a prompt or unblock a story. We chatted. We got to know each other while we worked. In a fully remote team, having room for that mattered too.

## You could leave

Being in the huddle didn’t mean giving it your full attention all day.

People left for meetings, concentration, breaks, or whatever else they needed to do. They joined again when it suited them.

That freedom was important. A compulsory day-long call would have been a very different experience. This didn’t feel like having to remain visibly engaged in a meeting.

We also didn’t do it every day. Some days were meeting-heavy. Sometimes people were on holiday. The pod formed when enough of us were around and wanted to work that way.

## Keeping the useful part

These huddles had happened on and off for months, but really took hold during a series of security incidents.

Some lasted into the evening. That wasn’t good, and it isn’t the part I would recommend. It was a crisis.

What interested me was that we continued the following week, during ordinary work. We kept getting together without needing every task to be urgent or everyone to be doing the same thing.

The useful part was having each other there, not staying late.

## An open door

The tooling didn’t always make this easy.

Sometimes two separate conversations developed and splitting the huddle made sense. But leaving to find or create another channel was awkward, particularly when we were already in the team’s development channel.

DMs and private channels brought other difficulties. Huddles became less discoverable, and we encountered restrictions around who could contribute to the chat.

When everyone is remote, you can’t see a few colleagues gathered around a desk and wander over. There needs to be some way to find the conversation.

I wanted smaller huddles that were still visible to the team. A PM should be able to find us and ask to join with a question. People should be able to splinter off, then return easily.

That doesn’t mean every conversation needs to be public, or that everyone must always be available. But everyday collaboration shouldn’t become a secret club.

Secret huddles aren’t particularly friendly.

## More of this, when it suits

I’m not proposing another ceremony or a rule that everyone should spend their day on a call.

I liked having colleagues nearby, even though we were physically apart. Sometimes we worked on one problem, sometimes on several, with agents doing useful work and people sharing what they were learning.

It felt more like being a team. And it was enjoyable without being exhausting.

That seems worth making room for.
